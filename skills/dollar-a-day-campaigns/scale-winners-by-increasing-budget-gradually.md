# Scale winners by increasing budget gradually

A small ad test has done well. You want to reach more people without losing control of the cost. This guide helps you choose the next step and know when to cut back.

**The path:** Qualified winner → bounded next spend → fresh check → keep or step back.

**Use this when:** A full-window winner meets the written goal and a longer run or budget increase is within the campaign’s authority.

## Inputs
- Winner IDs, full-window report, goal/target and evidence of useful result quality.
- Current and proposed budget, currency, dates, remaining aggregate commitments and the scope for increases or rollback.
- Adjustment log, reporting delay, decision window and supported platform controls; an actual operator or configured rule for the due review.

## First-run prompt

> Check this winner and propose the next dated budget step. Distinguish a $30 total over 30 days from $30 per day. Keep any increase within the source’s no-more-than-two-times ceiling and the lower actual authorized limit. Define the recheck and step-back rule before making any authorized change.

## Steps
1. Requalify the winner using the full test window, matched goal and result quality. A single cheap day or a low-count result does not establish performance at a larger budget.
2. Use the current owned method’s next stage where appropriate: $30 total over 30 days for a proven winner. This extends a small run; it is not $30/day and does not itself authorize new spend. Check whether the platform’s supported controls can enforce that plan.
3. If a separate larger-budget test is warranted and authorized, write the proposed amount and dates first. Preserve the source task’s ceiling of no more than 2× per adjustment, or a lower campaign limit. This is a method guardrail, not a claim that doubling is always safe or avoids learning changes.
4. Change one planned budget variable at a time and record the exact before/after state. Keep creative and targeting stable where possible. Do not raise a budget, widen audiences and extend dates together without recording the new scope and comparison limits.
5. Set a dated recheck window before the change, allowing for the account’s actual reporting delay and enough evidence for the chosen goal. Continue early spend and delivery checks; do not schedule automatic repeated doublings.
6. If the comparable cost per useful result crosses the written target after an increase, step back to the last supported budget within the agreed rollback authority, or pause if required by the stop rule. Verify tracking and lag; do not declare permanent audience exhaustion from one noisy period.
7. Watch actual frequency, useful response and cost together. Higher repeat exposure with weaker response is a reason to inspect fatigue, not a universal numeric frequency cutoff. A new audience is a separate switch test with its own budget, not guaranteed cheaper reach.
8. Read back any change and log the next review. Keep uncommitted money unspent. The older 90/10 allocation language is not permission to fund unproven paid content or exceed the budget; maintain a source-backed organic pipeline for future small tests.

## Definition of done (QA checklist)

- [ ] The winner and proposed next stage are supported by dated evidence.
- [ ] $30 over 30 days and all daily/total units are unambiguous.
- [ ] Any increase is at most 2× and within the actual lower authorized limit.
- [ ] Recheck and cost-crossing rollback rules have an owner and recorded state.
- [ ] Frequency, uncertainty and total commitments are tracked without guaranteed-learning claims.

## Example(s)

**Fictional teaching example:** An ad spends $7 for seven useful visits against a $2 target. A $30 total, 30-day continuation is approved and recorded as such. Later, a separate budget test raises a supported daily setting from $2 to $3, a 1.5× change. After the planned comparable window and tracking check, cost rises to $2.40 per useful visit. The operator uses the agreed rollback to $2 and reads it back. The report does not say that all 1.5× changes are safe or that the higher cost proves the audience is exhausted.
