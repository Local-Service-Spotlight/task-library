# Step 8: Add photos and featured image

A good picture can make a hard point easy to grasp. This guide helps you choose real proof or a clear diagram for your page. Start with the point you want the reader to see.

**The path:** Point to show → Real source or honest diagram → Clear placement → Phone and desktop check.

**Use this when:** The article needs a meaningful lead visual, useful supporting media and a suitable featured image.

## Inputs
- The full article and its opening/visual plan, with the source recording and real media library.
- Rights and identity/context for photos, screenshots or figures; private information that must be removed.
- The existing featured image and site media rules, plus access to its supported editor/media library.
- An image editing tool, actual target layout, and a saved original for any resized asset.

## First-run prompt

> Choose and place meaningful source-based visuals for this article. Keep real proof distinct from conceptual diagrams, preserve privacy and image proportions, and write useful alt text. Check the lead at desktop and phone sizes, or record that check as pending. Do not invent photos, results or a guaranteed load time.

## Steps
1. List the points that need a visual explanation or real proof. Prioritize one meaningful asset in the first desktop and phone screen. Add supporting visuals where they improve understanding rather than decorating every heading.
2. Choose a relevant real photo, permitted source-video frame, actual screenshot or evidence-backed diagram. Do not present stock or generated people as the subject or as proof of work. Clearly identify a conceptual diagram as such.
3. Verify who/what is shown and the right to use it. Remove exposed private data from the published copy while keeping the original protected. Do not alter a screenshot to imply a result that was not observed.
4. Select an article-specific featured image. Check existing usage and relevance; retain a suitable canonical image unless the task calls for a replacement. If a new choice is needed, avoid a generic duplicate hero that makes different articles indistinguishable.
5. Crop without cutting off the key person, text or evidence. Resize to the actual display needs and compress while checking legibility. File size alone cannot guarantee a three-second page load; measure the rendered page under stated conditions later.
6. Use a descriptive filename. Give informative images alt text that conveys their purpose. Decorative elements can have empty alt text; do not add keyword-stuffed descriptions or repeat a long caption word for word.
7. Place each asset beside its explanation. Caption real proof with known identity and context, and link the actual source where useful. Keep diagram labels short and readable; preserve image proportions on phone and desktop.
8. Save the draft and inspect the actual layout at 1440×860 and 390×844 when available. Confirm the lead’s meaningful content is visible, not merely its border, and nothing overflows. Record any pending rendered check and give the next editor the asset/source map.

## Definition of done (QA checklist)

- [ ] The lead visual explains the topic and has a real source or honest conceptual label.
- [ ] Rights, identity and private-data handling are checked.
- [ ] Images remain legible, proportionate and accessible in their actual placement.
- [ ] Featured-image choice and desktop/mobile evidence are recorded without a load-speed guarantee.

## Example(s)

**Fictional teaching example — no real photos or results.** The sample quote guide uses a conceptual diagram: “Whole bike photo → Close-up of issue → Note on when it happens.” Its caption explains that these inputs help a shop prepare; it does not claim they prove a diagnosis.

If a real source photo later shows a mechanic holding a bike wheel, an accurate alt text might describe that scene. It would not say “best cheap bike repair quote” unless those words genuinely describe the image’s purpose. A narrow phone crop that hides the damaged part fails even if the file is small.

Once for each article’s visual pass, then after a relevant layout or source-image change. No recurring image replacement is implied.
