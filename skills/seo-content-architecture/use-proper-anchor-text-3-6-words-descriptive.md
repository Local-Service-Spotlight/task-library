# Use proper anchor text (3–6 words, descriptive)

Help readers know where a link will take them. This guide replaces vague link words with clear names and useful phrases. Start with the links you plan to change and read each page they open.

**The path:** Anchor and context list → Destination promise check → Natural wording → Rendered link review

**Start when:** A draft or bounded existing-page audit contains vague, misleading or needlessly padded link text.

## Inputs

- The current source pages and a bounded anchor report, with exact href, surrounding sentence and source revision.
- The real destination content and [Goals, Content, Targeting: the result, source material and people served](https://blitzmetrics.com/gct-business-strategy/), plus [Choose the right entity destination](https://local-service-spotlight.github.io/task-library/?task=follow-entity-linking-decision-tree#task-follow-entity-linking-decision-tree) for identity and owned-explanation routes.
- The supported content source/builder, edit authority and relevant existing media/IDs to preserve.

## Steps

1. List the actual links in scope and read their surrounding copy. Flag vague words such as “click here,” a bare URL in ordinary explanatory prose, misleading names or excessive keyword repetition. Keep functional citations, legal identifiers and platform controls in their proper context instead of blindly replacing every URL string.
2. Read each destination and state what the reader gets there. Check that its person, company, topic or action matches the link’s promise. A link that opens a different page needs a destination decision before better wording can make it useful.
3. Write a concise descriptive phrase, usually three to six words when natural. Use the actual entity name where it is clearer, even if shorter. This is a house writing preference, not a Google minimum or maximum, and padding “Dennis Yu” into a longer phrase can make the reference less accurate.
4. Place the wording in a sentence that explains why the next page helps. Keep it natural for the author and reader. Reuse a correct name consistently where appropriate; do not force synonyms merely to make anchors look varied, and do not overload a phrase with search terms.
5. Preserve the difference between an owned explanation, a primary evidence citation and a necessary account or download action. Label the exact purpose and use its appropriate destination. A general method link cannot fulfill a button’s promise to open the real sign-in page.
6. Apply the narrow wording edit through the maintained source and inspect the result. Ensure that the clickable span includes the intended words, punctuation remains correct, and surrounding text, media and identifiers are unchanged. Check keyboard use and responsive layout for any affected link or button.
7. After authorized release, open the normal page and follow the edited link. Save the exact before/after anchor, href, revision and public evidence. Report resolved, held and intentionally retained cases within the stated scope; do not claim a whole-site cleanup from a sample or a failed fetch.

## Definition of done (QA checklist)

Quality assurance (QA) means checking the actual result against its agreed requirements. Follow the [Article Guidelines](https://localservicespotlight.com/article-guidelines/).

- [ ] Each revised phrase predicts the actual destination and fits the surrounding sentence.
- [ ] House word-count preferences do not distort real names, necessary actions or useful citations.
- [ ] Exact source and public anchor checks are saved, and scope limits and exceptions are explicit.
- [ ] The exact output, source revision, reviewer evidence and remaining owner action are saved.
- [ ] For any reader-facing output, the short grade-five opening states the reader’s useful outcome and supporting method or proof. The body delivers that promise; a useful authentic visual appears in the first screen. Retain exact text and quoted reviewer evidence.

## Example(s)

**Fictional teaching example. This is not a client result or proof of a completed run.**

A fictional repair guide says “click here” before a link to its leak test method. The editor changes it to “check a roof leak” and reads the target to confirm the promise. A nearby link named for a two-word person remains that person’s actual name. Both are clear, even though only one uses the usual three-to-six-word phrase.
