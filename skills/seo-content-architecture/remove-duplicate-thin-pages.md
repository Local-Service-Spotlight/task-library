# Remove duplicate thin pages

Keep your best page and the useful facts it holds. This guide checks weak or repeated pages and plans what to keep, join or remove. Start with the pages that seem to serve the same need and read what each one adds.

**The path:** Suspect URLs → Intent and evidence review → Keep or change proposal → Guarded release checks

**Start when:** The content inventory has identified possible duplicate intent or pages with little useful original value.

## Inputs

- [Inventory pages and their purpose](https://local-service-spotlight.github.io/task-library/?task=step-1-inventory-content-and-establish-gct-per-page#task-step-1-inventory-content-and-establish-gct-per-page) and [Map actual content roles](https://local-service-spotlight.github.io/task-library/?task=map-content-hierarchically-root-trunk-branches-leaves#task-map-content-hierarchically-root-trunk-branches-leaves) with current roles, links and possible overlap.
- Exact current source, media, search/referral data when available, business function and useful facts for every affected URL.
- The existing source/redirect owner, recovery copy and explicit authority for the exact consolidation, deletion or route change.

## Steps

1. Treat similar titles, low word counts and repeated phrases as review signals, not verdicts. Read each page’s actual promise, audience, method, proof and business function. A short contact page, service page and detailed how-to can all be useful; shared terms do not make them interchangeable.
2. Identify the owning intent and any genuinely equivalent URLs. List unique facts, examples, links, media, attribution, comments or conversion functions that a change must preserve. Review available search and referral evidence with its period and limits; missing measurement is unknown, not proof that a page has no value.
3. Choose keep, improve, merge, canonical-review or remove for each exact URL, with a reason and owner. A canonical hint can identify a preferred duplicate representation but does not itself delete content or guarantee the selected search URL. Avoid turning every overlapping story into a competing-hub problem.
4. For a merge, place the useful unique material into the true equivalent destination and review the new whole page. Preserve source ownership, relevant media and claims; do not copy unsupported assertions merely because they existed in the old draft. Verify that the replacement serves what old visitors expected.
5. Prepare a precise route map. Use a permanent redirect when content has moved to a relevant replacement; if removed content has no equivalent, an appropriate 404 or 410 may be correct. An unrelated closest-hub redirect can mislead readers and be treated as a soft error. Never create a redirect loop or chain as a shortcut.
6. Apply authorized source and routing changes in the agreed sequence with current-source guards and recovery evidence. Update controlled internal links to the final relevant targets and verify generated sitemap behavior through the maintained tool. A sitemap refresh or canonical tag does not guarantee Google has reprocessed the change.
7. Check the normal old and new URLs, actual content/media, statuses, canonical signals and important page functions. Re-crawl affected links and record what changed, what remains pending and the observation date. Pass the accepted change set to measurement; do not claim that consolidation automatically raised rankings.

## Example(s)

**Fictional teaching example. This is not a client result or proof of a completed run.**

A fictional site has two live pages for the same course and a short contact page. One course page contains a unique teacher interview, so the team preserves it in the main course page before proposing a redirect. The contact page remains because it serves a different need. The report records a reviewed consolidation, not three automatic deletions.
