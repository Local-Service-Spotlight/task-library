# Turn your old footage into useful evidence of your work

By Dennis Yu · November 10, 2026

You may already have the footage you need. It is on your phone, in cloud photo albums, inside an old editing project, or on a channel you have not opened in years.

I have years of conference footage in iCloud and Google Photos. Some of it includes my narration about what to capture before, during and after a talk. The value comes from finding the right moment and making something useful with it.

Give an agent a specific assignment: find a real example of a founder explaining a particular problem to the people they serve. That is a better starting point than asking it to turn the whole archive into content.

## Start with one person and one question

Choose the founder, the audience, and the question you want to answer. You might need a reel for an organizer, a short explanation for a prospect, or an article that shows how a problem was solved.

Search by person, event, topic, place and approximate date. Look at neighboring files. The explanation recorded in a hallway may be more useful than the stage photo beside it.

Ask the agent to inspect what it finds. A filename, thumbnail or transcript match is a lead. It becomes a source when the agent can open the file and verify the relevant moment.

## Keep the source attached

For each useful file, save the original reference, recording date if known, event, speaker, length and reuse terms. Keep the selected time range with every quotation.

An upload date may be years later than the recording date. A stage backdrop may help identify an event, but an agenda or organizer record gives you another way to check it. Leave uncertain details marked unknown until you can resolve them.

Do not delete alternate angles because they look like duplicates. Exact file matches can be grouped after export, while originals remain available. Different edits and camera views may serve different purposes.

## Match the evidence to the claim

A video of a founder speaking demonstrates that appearance. A clear explanation can show expertise. Specific participant feedback can show what someone took away.

A photo beside a famous person does not establish that the founder worked with them. A full room does not establish a revenue result. Keep the claim as precise as the evidence.

That discipline makes the article more useful. A reader can follow the source and decide what it supports.

## Make an article that adds something

Use a transcript to find the lesson, then verify the words against the recording. Explain the problem, the method, and the limitations. Include the relevant clip and a still that helps the reader understand it.

Label older footage honestly. A dated example can show how a method developed. Recheck advice that may have changed, including software steps, prices and platform rules.

Give each derivative a purpose. The reel shows delivery and fit. The short answers one question. The article supplies the context and instructions. The speaker page brings the relevant evidence together.

## Finish a small package before expanding

Choose one event package. Produce a reel, a few useful lessons and an article. Check names, captions, sound, crops, links and the booking action. Record where the finished assets landed.

Then measure what matters: how long it took to finish, whether the assets passed review, and whether qualified inquiries or bookings followed. Monitor search visibility and AI citations as observed results, with the query and date attached.

Google's published guidance emphasizes useful content and clear evidence of experience. It does not promise that an archive, a reel or structured data will cause a recommendation. Your old footage gives you material for helping people assess your work. The agent's job is to find it, preserve the context, and finish the useful output.
