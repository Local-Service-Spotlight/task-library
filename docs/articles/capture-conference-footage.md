# What to film when you speak at a conference

By Dennis Yu · November 10, 2026

When I speak at a conference, I want footage of the lesson and the people using it. A close shot of my face tells only part of that story. The room, the questions, and what happens afterward help someone understand the work.

I have been collecting phone footage for years, including narration about what I am filming and why. The next step is to make that method easy for a founder and their agent to repeat.

Start with the asset you want to create. A speaker reel helps an organizer decide whether you fit their event. A teaching clip helps a viewer solve a problem. A three-minute training video can show your operator how the whole process works. Those videos need different edits, even when they use the same source footage.

## Protect the full talk

Confirm what the organizer permits before recording or reusing footage. Ask about the official recording, audio feed, slides, and delivery date. Then test your own permitted setup.

Put one phone where it can see you clearly and capture useful audio. Check battery and storage before the room fills. Record the host's introduction and keep the talk running.

If that is your only camera, leave it in place. Get the venue shots before the session and your debrief afterward. When you have another operator or camera, use that one to collect the other angles.

## Get the angles that tell the story

From the back of the room, show the speaker and the audience together. From the side, show gestures and interaction. Get a closer view of a complete explanation or demonstration. Hold each shot steady long enough for an editor to use it.

Capture real audience participation where permitted. Keep it tied to the moment that caused it. Moving applause from one event under a line from another can make a reel look stronger while making it less trustworthy.

The introduction is useful because it records what the host actually said. Keep the wording and context. It does not establish every claim you might want to put in a bio.

## Stay after the session

Ask a participant what they will use from the talk. A specific answer is useful to the next organizer and to the people who could not attend.

Record a short debrief while the experience is fresh. What question surprised you? What did you explain differently? What should someone do next?

My older speaking playbook already emphasizes the value of the time after a talk and keeping contacts and presentations in a Content Library. The filming process should preserve that part of the experience, too.

## Finish something with the footage

Save the originals with the event, date, source and reuse terms. Index the useful moments by timecode. Choose a complete teaching point before adding music or transitions.

For a first reel, try a strong teaching line, enough context to identify the event, another useful moment, specific feedback if available, and one booking action. A short first cut can test the story before you build a longer version.

Use the same source to make a separate teaching clip. Write an article that explains the lesson and links to the relevant video. Add useful stills with accurate captions. Put the reel on the speaker page so an organizer can find it.

This makes your work easier to assess. It does not guarantee a ranking or a recommendation from an AI system. The goal is to help a real person see what you know and decide whether you can help them.

At the next event, give one person the capture card, protect the full recording, and finish one reel and one useful lesson. That is a repeatable process.
