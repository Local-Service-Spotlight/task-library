# Conference capture to speaker reel

Version 1.0 · November 10, 2026 · Dennis Yu training companion

A founder should leave a speaking engagement with evidence of what they taught, who they helped, and where it happened. This companion turns that evidence into a useful speaker reel, teaching clips, and articles. It extends the existing conference-filming task and speaking guide. Keep the original recordings.

## Start with the outcome

Build two different videos. The three-minute training film teaches an operator what to collect and shows the route from raw clips to finished assets. The speaker reel helps an organizer assess a speaker. A useful first reel can run 60–90 seconds; choose its length for its audience and evidence, rather than forcing every asset to three minutes.

A real appearance establishes that a person spoke at a particular event. The substance of the talk can demonstrate expertise. An introduction documents what the host said. None of these alone proves business results, an endorsement by every attendee, or that a search engine will recommend the person.

## The capture card

Before the session, confirm the organizer's recording and reuse rules. Test storage, battery, framing and audio. Confirm how to obtain any permitted official video, audio feed and slides. Record the event name, session title and date from an agenda or organizer source.

| Scene | Position and action | Capture target | What it contributes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Venue and session | Entrance/signage, then agenda/session title | Two steady 5–10-second shots; avoid private badge details | Verifiable event context |
| Before the talk | Backstage or quiet hallway | One 20–30-second explanation of whom the talk helps and what to watch for | Purpose and behind-the-scenes teaching |
| Host introduction | Fixed camera facing host and stage | Complete permitted introduction | Exact third-party wording, with context |
| Full talk master | Stable front/center or clear diagonal position | Continuous permitted talk; keep speaker visible and voice intelligible | Source for teaching clips and accurate quotations |
| Wide room view | Back of room, over shoulders toward stage | 5–10 seconds per shot with speaker and room in frame | Scale and setting, only to the extent visible |
| Side angle | Stage side or side aisle where allowed | Stable speaker profile plus audience or screen | Delivery, gestures and a second editing angle |
| Teaching detail | Closer view from an allowed position | A complete 20–60-second explanation, demonstration or example | Actual expertise and a standalone lesson |
| Audience response | Cleared audience from side/rear | A genuine reaction or participation with its original context | Engagement without manufactured applause |
| Question and answer | Cleared questioner or speaker-only answer | Complete question/answer when permitted; identify missing context | Real audience needs and follow-up topics |
| After the talk | Quiet hallway or approved signing area | Specific attendee/organizer feedback, with reuse permission | Attributable feedback about a particular takeaway |
| Speaker debrief | Quiet location after the session | 20–30 seconds on what worked, what changed, and the next action | Reflection and an article conclusion |

Durations are capture targets, not claims about files already recorded. Hold a shot still for a few seconds before and after the action. Frame wide enough to crop thoughtfully. When possible, collect deliberate horizontal and vertical shots; do not assume a crop will preserve the speaker, slides and audience together.

## One phone or two

With one phone, prioritize the complete talk and useful audio. Collect venue, backstage and debrief shots before and after. A second angle can come from permitted official footage. Do not walk the only camera around the room and later call its output a complete recording.

With two phones or an operator plus official footage, keep one recording continuous. Use the other for room, side, audience and detail shots. Label the sources and match timing with a shared visible/audible moment if possible. A borrowed applause clip must never imply a reaction to a line that did not cause it.

Ask the attendee a specific question: “What idea from this session will you use, and how?” Preserve their own answer. A pleasant photo or generic praise does not establish a measured business result.

## The three-minute teaching film

This is the target storyboard for the complete new demonstration. The existing narration recut is a separate asset. Fill the storyboard with reviewed real footage; missing shots remain a visible production gap.

| Time | Show | Narration to record or select |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 00:00–00:15 | Finished reel excerpt, then its source shots | “When you speak at a conference, collect enough footage to show what happened and what you taught. Here are the shots I want, and how they become a speaker reel.” |
| 00:15–00:35 | Venue, agenda and backstage context | “Start before the talk. Record the event and your session title. Get a short explanation of whom you are helping. Keep the date and source with the files.” |
| 00:35–01:00 | Camera position and real audio setup | “Keep one camera steady for the full talk. Check the sound before the room fills. With one phone, protect that recording. With a second camera, collect the other angles.” |
| 01:00–01:35 | Front, back, side and close views, labeled | “Get a clear front view. From the back, show the speaker and the room together. From the side, show delivery and participation. Move closer for a useful explanation. Hold each shot still.” |
| 01:35–01:55 | Host introduction, real reaction and Q&A | “Save the host's introduction and the permitted questions. Keep reactions tied to what happened. Each useful answer can become its own teaching video.” |
| 01:55–02:15 | Specific feedback and speaker debrief | “Afterward, ask what someone will use from the session. Record a short debrief while the lesson is fresh. Keep permission with any feedback you plan to publish.” |
| 02:15–02:40 | Originals, transcript and source-linked timeline | “Save the originals. Give every clip its event, source and timecode. Choose a strong teaching moment, add the room and the second angle, and use feedback only for what it actually says.” |
| 02:40–03:00 | Finished reel, vertical lesson, article and speaker page | “Now use the same evidence in a reel, a short lesson and an article that answers the question. Link the article to the video and your speaking page. Track the inquiries and bookings that follow.” |

The timeline totals 180 seconds. This is proposed narration, not a verbatim transcript or a record of completed filming. Use Dennis's actual narration where it fits. If the words or coverage are absent, record them later or revise the segment. Do not synthesize a testimonial, event, audience or source quotation to fill a gap.

## Build the speaker reel

A suggested 75-second first cut:

| Reel time | Editorial job | Source requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 00:00–00:08 | Open with a useful, complete speaker line | Exact source and time range |
| 00:08–00:18 | Establish the event and speaker | Correct name/title, event/date, wide view or agenda |
| 00:18–00:43 | Show two complementary teaching/delivery moments | Complete thoughts with enough context; no unsupported result claims |
| 00:43–01:00 | Add specific third-party feedback or a real exchange | Attribution and permission; omit if unavailable |
| 01:00–01:10 | Show delivery and participation from another view | Match the real event; label archival material if mixed |
| 01:10–01:15 | Close with one booking action | Working speaker page or contact route |

If feedback is missing, use more substantive teaching. Do not invent a testimonial to satisfy the template. Let speech carry the video. Music is optional, needs proper rights, and must not mask the voice. Keep faces natural; avoid edits that distort expressions or create distracting jump cuts.

## Recover ten years of scattered footage

Create an index before a mass migration. Start with the next useful output: one founder, one audience, one topic, one booking or client outcome.

1. Search the connected iCloud/Google Photos sources, approved Drive folders, existing editing projects, Zoom recordings, published channels and permitted event archives. Access to one service does not establish access to another.
2. Search by person, event, location, topic and approximate date. Check neighboring clips: the useful explanation may be adjacent to the stage shot.
3. Log each original's stable source ID/URL, filename, capture date/timezone, event, speaker, duration, dimensions, audio/transcript status, rights and topics. Mark uncertain fields unknown.
4. Compare hashes after originals are exported to identify exact duplicates. Preserve alternate edits and different camera angles. Keep originals; work from copies.
5. Transcribe relevant sources and index complete teaching moments. Retain the source time range for every quotation and derivative.
6. Select a small event package and finish it through playback, article review and destination verification. Expand only after the process works.

## Repurpose by purpose

| Output | What a person should get | Evidence and destination |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full talk | Complete session with visible coverage gaps | Original recording; private preservation or approved public channel |
| Speaker reel | Confidence about topic, delivery and event fit | 60–90-second first cut; speaker page and organizer media kit |
| Teaching clips | One answer or demonstration per clip | Source-linked 20–60-second cuts; suitable vertical/horizontal versions |
| Practical article | A clear answer supported by a real example | Exact video embed/time range, event context, useful stills and author |
| Event recap | What happened, what was taught and useful next steps | Correct agenda, event/date and actual footage |
| Book/workbook insertion | A reusable lesson and exercise | Reviewed passage and durable companion link; update existing source |

Reuse the lesson in context. Do not flood channels with the same generic summary. Use transcripts as working material, then verify names, numbers and meaning against the source.

## Acceptance checks

- Original files remain available and coverage gaps are recorded.
- Speaker identity, event, recording date and reuse scope have evidence.
- Every scene and quote resolves to an original and time range.
- The 180-second training cut and the speaker reel have distinct purposes.
- Captions, names, crops, transitions and the booking action are checked.
- Playback and audio review state the exact review performed. Metadata and waveforms alone do not establish intelligible speech.
- Articles answer a useful question and retain source links, accurate attribution and relevant images.
- Exported file, review link and public URL are recorded separately.
- Public availability is verified at the destination before marking published.
- Measure source-to-finished turnaround, accepted assets, qualified inquiries and bookings. Treat observed search/AI citations as outcomes to monitor, not promised results.
